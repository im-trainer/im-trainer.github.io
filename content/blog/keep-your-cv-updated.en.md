> I'm not looking for a job, so why would I update my CV?

I hear this question a lot, from students and from people with many years of experience. The short answer: because you don't get to pick the moment you'll need it 🙂.

## ⏳ The "safe" job and relationships that take time

A job feels safe until it isn't: the project changes, the team changes, the company changes, or simply what you want changes. Even if you can't picture yourself looking for something else today, that can change at any time.

You can write a CV in one evening. You can't do that with relationships. They are built over time, and so is the network side of your public profiles. Connect with your colleagues while you still work together, help where you can, recommend good people. You can't ask for an "urgent" recommendation from someone who hasn't heard from you in five years.

## 📝 Keep an achievements journal

The hardest part of updating a CV is remembering what you did. Two years from now, the details of today's project will be gone.

The fix is simple: one file where, every 2–3 months, you note what you delivered, what you learned and what problem you solved. It takes 10 minutes. When the moment comes, the CV almost writes itself, and the notes help with your yearly review too.

## 🎯 Results, not responsibilities

Compare two lines from the same CV:

- ❌ "Responsible for the build process."
- ✅ "Reduced build time from 10 minutes to 3."

The first one says what was in the job description. The second one says what **you** did. Add numbers where you have them; where you don't, describe what changed after your work.

## 👀 First impressions: the first lines matter

There's a popular myth that "the robot rejects my CV". Most of the time the CV still reaches a person, but that person reads fast, sometimes in a few seconds. First impressions matter, which is why the first lines have to say what value you bring.

One more important thing: **a CV is not a complete archive**. Keeping it updated doesn't mean adding to it forever. Remove what is no longer relevant, so the things that matter today are visible right away.

## ✨ Rememberable, without bragging

Many people avoid writing about what they did so they don't seem to be bragging. But presenting the truth is not bragging: what you did and what you enjoy doing. Sometimes that is exactly what motivates others, and it makes you stand out in a pile of CVs that all look the same.

The rule that keeps you away from bragging is honesty: **write only what you can back up**. Any line in your CV can become an interview question. And "currently learning React" is a perfectly good line, much better than a "React: advanced" you can't defend.

## 🚀 Personal projects and enthusiasm

Talk with enthusiasm about what you're working on, even if it's a personal project. In an interview it can count for more than your day-to-day work, because it was your choice, not a task you were given.

When you talk about a project, answer three questions:

1. What problem does it solve?
2. How does it help you, personally?
3. What did you learn building it?

This shows your motivation to study and to solve problems, and that is what the interviewer is really looking for.

Are you a student with no experience yet? Course projects, volunteering, a hackathon or a small contribution to an open-source project are experience. Write them down.

## 🔗 CV = LinkedIn = GitHub

Whoever reads your CV will also look you up online. Make sure they find the same story everywhere:

- fill in your LinkedIn profile and [personalize its URL](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/87/customizing-your-public-profile-url?lang=en);
- always keep your "About" section current: a few sentences about who you are, what you do now and what you enjoy;
- choose a picture that is serious, but "rememberable";
- link your accounts together: LinkedIn, GitHub and your CV should point to each other;
- use GitHub as proof: a project with a clear README and a live link (for example, through [GitHub Pages](https://pages.github.com/)) says more than a list of technologies.

## ✅ The 15-minute checklist

You don't have to do everything today. Start with this:

- [ ] Open your CV and add what you did in the last 6 months.
- [ ] Remove one thing that is no longer relevant.
- [ ] Rewrite one responsibility as a result.
- [ ] Add one personal project, with a link and one sentence about the problem it solves.
- [ ] Check that LinkedIn (including "About") and GitHub tell the same story as your CV.
- [ ] Connect with three colleagues or former colleagues.
- [ ] Set a reminder 3 months from now to do it again.

> **Relationships are built over time, not all at once.** Keep your CV updated while you don't need it yet.
>
